How the modelling works

The chain. The sun throws out magnetised plasma (the solar wind). When it hits Earth's magnetic field it drives currents that dump energy into the upper atmosphere. The aurora is that energy exciting air molecules at 100-300 km altitude.

Data sources. The dashboard stitches together five live feeds:

  • NOAA SWPC real-time solar wind — magnetic field (Bz, Bt) and plasma (speed, density) measured at the L1 Lagrange point, 1.5 million km upstream. Updated every minute.
  • Kp index — the planet-wide magnetic activity level in 3-hour bins. Observed, estimated, and forecast up to 3 days ahead.
  • OVATION — a 30-minute probability-of-visibility map from DMSP satellites. Used for the first ~1.5 h instead of the Kp-based model.
  • Open-Meteo and MET Norway — cloud cover forecasts in low / mid / high layers.

Per-hour score. For every hour of the forecast, at your location:

P(aurora) = platt( P_geo_raw ) × P_clear × darkness × moon_factor
  • P_geo_raw — geometric probability the auroral oval is overhead or nearby. Uses the current forecast Kp to place the oval's equatorward edge in corrected geomagnetic latitude (AACGM-v2). Roughly: edge ≈ 66.5° − 2°·Kp, with a magnetic-local-time shift that pushes the oval ~3-5° further south around magnetic midnight and further north toward magnetic noon. A sigmoid ±1.5° wide blurs the hard boundary, plus a weaker "on the horizon" contribution from 2.5° further south. Capped at 0.8. A poleward edge cuts the score to ~0 if you're in the polar cap.
  • platt(·) — a 2-parameter calibration, σ(1.98·logit(P) − 1.79), fitted against 20,806 station-hours of IMAGE magnetometer activity across 10 Fennoscandian stations, Sep–Nov 2024. Monotone, so ordering (AUC 0.91) is untouched — only the dial is rescaled. Brier drops from 0.14 (raw) to 0.09 against a climatology baseline of 0.15. Before this, the model was ~2× over-confident in the mid-range; after it, the number on the dial matches observed frequencies.
  • P_clear = 1 − max(low, mid, ½·high) cloud fraction. Low cloud blocks everything; high cloud is thin cirrus that aurora can shine through.
  • darkness — linear ramp from 0 at sun altitude −6° (civil twilight) to 1 at −12° (nautical twilight).
  • moon_factor — 1 if the moon is below the horizon, else 1 − 0.5·illuminated_fraction·sin(alt)/sin(30°). A full moon high in the sky halves your chance; a crescent at the horizon barely matters.

Corrected magnetic latitude (AACGM). Earth's field isn't a clean dipole — it's offset and tilted, and has a big dent in the North Atlantic. The dashboard uses AACGM-v2 coordinates at 110 km altitude, which trace the actual field line back to the magnetic equator. For Finland the correction is <1°, but for Iceland and Greenland it's 3-5° equatorward of what a centered-dipole model would suggest.

Confidence (0-1):

  • Short-term (next ~1.5 h, when OVATION is in play): 0.7
  • Tonight (next 6-12 h): 0.5 — Kp forecast skill drops fast after a day
  • Tomorrow night: 0.3 — mostly a wild guess unless there's an active CME
  • Cloud forecast at 24 h+: 0.4 — weather models drift more than magnetic ones

Calibration caveat. The ground truth was magnetometer activity (hourly X-component range ≥ 100 nT). Magnetometers under-count quiet or pulsating arcs — faint aurora can be visible overhead with no strong current signature. So the calibrated P is a *lower bound* on visible-aurora probability, especially at the top of the scale: observations show ~95% activity rate when the calibrated dial reads 70%. In practice, treat anything ≥ 60% as "going off", below 15% as climatological noise.

What this doesn't model: substorms (sudden auroral intensification, unpredictable), cusp aurora around magnetic noon (dayside, affects Svalbard mostly), polar-cap arcs during quiet northward Bz, light pollution, your horizon obstructions, and you going to sleep.
